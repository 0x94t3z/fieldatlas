Year 10 History - Unit 4 handout
The Peace of Westphalia (1648)

Learning objectives
- Describe the main terms of the Peace of Westphalia.
- Explain why historians link Westphalia with the idea of state sovereignty.
- Evaluate whether 1648 really was a turning point.

1. The negotiations
The peace was not one treaty but a set of agreements negotiated from 1643 to 1648 in two towns in Westphalia: Catholic delegations met in Münster and Protestant delegations met in Osnabrück, about 50 km apart. Envoys from the Holy Roman Emperor Ferdinand III, France, Sweden, Spain, the Dutch Republic and dozens of German princes took part. It was one of the first great European peace congresses.

2. Main terms
- The Treaty of Münster (January 1648) between Spain and the Dutch Republic ended the Eighty Years' War and recognised Dutch independence.
- The Treaties of Osnabrück and Münster (October 1648) ended the Thirty Years' War in the Holy Roman Empire.
- The principle of cuius regio, eius religio from the Peace of Augsburg was confirmed, but now Calvinism was recognised alongside Catholicism and Lutheranism.
- The "normal year" of 1624 was used to decide which territories counted as Catholic or Protestant.
- German princes were given the right to make alliances with foreign powers (as long as these were not against the Emperor).
- Sweden gained Western Pomerania and Bremen-Verden; France gained most of Alsace and confirmed control of Metz, Toul and Verdun.
- Switzerland's independence from the Empire was formally recognised.

3. "Westphalian sovereignty"
Political scientists use the phrase "Westphalian system" to describe a world of independent states that do not interfere in each other's internal affairs. Some historians argue this is a myth created later, because the treaties actually kept the Empire together and gave outside powers (France and Sweden) the right to guarantee the settlement.
