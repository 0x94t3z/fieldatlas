Notes from the Allied Museum visit (Berlin, half term) - typed up from the leaflet and the labels, for Nadia's project

The airlift
- Called Luftbrücke in German, literally "air bridge". Berliners called the planes Rosinenbomber, "raisin bombers", after the sweets some crews dropped by handkerchief parachute. The pilot who started that was Gail Halvorsen, "the Candy Bomber" / "Uncle Wiggly Wings".
- It ran from 26 June 1948 to 30 September 1949, although the blockade itself was lifted on 12 May 1949. The Allies kept flying for another four months to build up stockpiles in case it started again.
- Three air corridors into West Berlin, each 20 miles wide: from Hamburg, Bückeburg/Hannover and Frankfurt.
- Airfields used: Tempelhof (US), Gatow (British), and Tegel, which was built during the airlift itself in about 90 days, largely by Berlin women and rubble labour.
- Over 2.3 million tons delivered in total, two thirds of it coal. Roughly 277,000 flights.
- At the peak, the "Easter Parade" of 15-16 April 1949, nearly 13,000 tons landed in 24 hours, a plane touching down every minute or so.
- Aircraft: C-47 Dakotas at first, then mostly C-54 Skymasters which carried 10 tons. The British also used Sunderland flying boats landing on the Havel, which was useful for salt because their hulls were already corrosion-proofed.
- 101 people died in connection with the operation, including 40 British and 31 American personnel, mostly in crashes. There is a memorial at Tempelhof shaped like three curved arcs for the three corridors.
- Tunner's "block system" turned it into a conveyor belt: fixed altitudes, fixed intervals, crews not allowed to leave their aircraft, weather briefings and coffee brought to the plane, unload in under 30 minutes.
